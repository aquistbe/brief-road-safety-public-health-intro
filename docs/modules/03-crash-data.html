3 · Where crash data come from

~25 min · reading + quiz

What you’ll learn

  • The main sources of crash and injury data
  • How injury severity is recorded
  • What these data sources miss
  • Why studying one street is harder than it looks

1. Police crash reports

Most crash data start with a police report. An officer at the scene fills in a form: date, time, location, the people and vehicles involved, road and weather conditions, and how badly people seem to be hurt. States collect these reports into databases.

Police reports are the most complete source on where crashes happen, which makes them central to street-level research. But they’re filled in quickly, at the scene, by people who aren’t doctors.

2. National databases

Source What it covers Run by
FARS (Fatality Analysis Reporting System) Every crash on a public road in which someone died within 30 days. National data back to 1975. NHTSA
CRSS (Crash Report Sampling System) A national sample of police reports of all severities, used to estimate totals NHTSA
NEISS (National Electronic Injury Surveillance System) A sample of about 100 hospital emergency departments, used to estimate injuries nationally U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission
Hospital and trauma registries Detailed medical information on people treated States and hospitals

3. How severity is recorded: KABCO

Police usually record injuries on the KABCO scale:

Code Meaning
K Killed
A Suspected serious injury
B Suspected minor injury
C Possible injury
O No apparent injury

Researchers often combine K and A into KSI: killed or seriously injured.

4. What the data miss

  • Underreporting. Many crashes involving people walking or cycling, especially minor ones, never reach a police report. The person may go home, or straight to a hospital.
  • Severity guesses. An officer’s “A” or “B” at the scene can differ from what doctors later find.
  • Location errors. The location may be recorded at the nearest intersection, or not exactly.
  • Missing exposure. Crash data say nothing about how many people walked safely past the same spot (module 4).

Linking police reports to hospital records helps fill these gaps, but it’s hard to do.

5. Why one street is hard to study

Fatal crashes are, thankfully, rare on any single street. A block might have zero deaths for ten years, then one. Was the street dangerous, or unlucky? You can’t tell from one block.

That’s why researchers:

  • use KSI, or all injury crashes, not just deaths, to get more events;
  • study many streets at once, and compare streets with and without a feature;
  • use statistical methods that account for chance (module 4, regression to the mean).
NoteGo deeper (college)
  • FARS counts deaths within 30 days of the crash; other systems define deaths differently.
  • CRSS and NEISS are samples, so their national totals are estimates with uncertainty. FARS is a census.
  • Crash counts per street segment are usually modeled with negative binomial regression. Counts vary more than a simple Poisson model expects.

Why this matters for your work

Crash data tell us where people were hurt. Your labels tell us what the street looked like. The study needs both, linked carefully.
