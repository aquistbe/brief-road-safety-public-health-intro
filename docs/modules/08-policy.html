8 · From research to safer streets

~20 min · reading + reflection + quiz

What you’ll learn

  • Who uses pedestrian safety research, and how
  • How to read a safety map or dashboard carefully
  • Why the words we use matter
  • Careers in injury prevention and transportation safety

1. Who uses this research

Who What they do with it
City transportation departments Decide which streets to redesign first, and which features to use
Vision Zero programs Map the “high-injury network,” the small share of streets with most serious crashes, and track progress
State departments of transportation Fund safety projects; set design standards for state roads, which are often the most dangerous urban streets
Health departments Treat traffic injury as a public health problem; link it to physical activity, aging and equity
Advocates and community groups Push for changes on their own streets, using data as evidence
Researchers Test which features actually work, and for whom

Research rarely changes a street by itself. It becomes one piece of evidence that planners, residents and elected officials weigh along with cost, space and what the community wants.

2. Reading maps and dashboards carefully

Maps of crashes are powerful, and easy to misread. Use what you learned in module 4:

  • Counts or rates? A map of crash counts mostly shows where people and cars are.
  • Exposure: busy downtown streets will always look “red” on a count map.
  • Places, not people: an area-level map describes places, not individuals (the ecological fallacy).
  • Time period: a few years of data can be noisy. Was the period chosen because it was unusual?
  • What’s not built yet: a research dashboard may show early results. Read its notes about what’s demonstrated and what’s still a prototype.

3. Words matter

The words we use shape who gets blamed, and what gets fixed.

Instead of Try Why
“Accident” “Crash” Crashes are predictable and preventable (module 1)
“Pedestrian was hit by a car” “A driver struck a person walking” Names who was involved, not just the object
“Jaywalker” “Person crossing mid-block” Most deaths happen mid-block, often where no safe crossing is nearby (module 2)
“Pedestrian error” Describe the street: “no crossing for half a mile” Points to what can be changed

Accurate language also means not overstating results. Say “linked with” rather than “causes” unless the study design supports a causal claim.

4. Careers

People who work on this problem include:

  • Epidemiologists and biostatisticians at universities, health departments and the CDC’s injury prevention programs
  • Transportation engineers and planners at city and state departments of transportation
  • Data scientists building tools like the ones in this project
  • Policy analysts and advocates at nonprofits and government agencies
  • Emergency medicine and trauma professionals who treat injuries and study prevention

Useful college majors include public health, epidemiology, statistics, data science, civil engineering, urban planning, geography and computer science. Many of these fields welcome people from the others.
